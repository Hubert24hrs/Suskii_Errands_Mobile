# Audit — 2026-09-22 (fourth pass) — whole repository, both agents

The first three passes this week each audited one surface: the money, the RLS matrix, and then
whatever reviewing Kimi's M8.6 happened to expose. This one is the whole repository — 127,000
lines across both agents — and it is the first pass to run the frontend's own tooling rather than
reasoning about it, because `flutter`, `dart`, `node` and `uv` are all available on this machine
even though Docker is not.

**Five findings. Three are fixed here; two are Kimi's and are filed rather than fixed, for a
reason given in each case.**

The headline is not any individual finding. It is that **U.1 was declared closed and was not**:
the second pass fixed twenty-eight policies and stopped, because a person reading a matrix stops
where their attention does. The control built in this pass does not get tired, and it found two
more instances in its first thirty seconds.

## W.1 — `profiles` was readable by every admin, in every country (Medium–High, fixed)

**What.** The matrix gives Support, Verification, Finance and Dispute `R:scope` on `profiles` in
four separate columns, and super admin plain `R`. The policy asked `private.is_any_admin()`, which
is `has_admin_role(enum_range(...))` — every role, no scope at all.

**Consequence.** A support agent scoped to Nigeria read every profile on the platform: display
name, country, language, both verification standings, trust level. That is personal data about
every user in every country, and the DPIA is written on it being scoped.

**Why it survived U.1.** The second pass rewrote twenty-eight policies and this was not one of
them. `profiles` is in section 2 of the matrix, away from the marketplace and money tables the
pass was concentrating on.

## W.2 — `approvals` was readable by every admin role (Medium, fixed)

**What.** The matrix says "requester R:own; approvers R:scope". The policy said requester or
`is_any_admin()`.

**Consequence.** A verification officer — who approves nothing — read every four-eyes approval in
every country, which is a ledger of who is taking money out and when.

**Fix.** `approvals` is polymorphic in the same way `fraud_flags` is (`subject_kind` plus a text
id), so reaching a country needs a resolver rather than a column.
`private.approval_subject_country` does the switch once for the two kinds anything writes —
`withdrawal` and `config_change` — and access narrows to `finance_officer` plus super admin. A
global config change resolves to no country and only super admin reads it, which is the
fail-closed rule everywhere else and is right here: a change with no country is a change to
everybody's platform.

## W.3 — a provider could read the customer's checkout URL (Medium, latent, fixed)

**What.** The matrix gives a job participant `R:part (status, amount)` on `payments`. The grant
covered all twenty-one columns, and `private.is_job_participant` includes the provider.

**Consequence.** The provider on a job could read `payments.checkout_url` — the customer's hosted
payment page. That is a **capability, not a reference**: whoever holds it can open the page.

**Why it is only latent.** `checkout_url` is NULL today and stays NULL until a real gateway exists
(client action 4). That is precisely the argument for fixing it now, while the column is empty and
no client reads it, rather than after M9 when closing it would be a breaking change against a
wired app.

**Fix.** The column is revoked from `authenticated` and the payer reaches it through
`public.get_payment_checkout(request_id)`, which checks who is asking. The other twenty columns
stay readable, because the matrix's "(status, amount)" is narrower than the product needs — a
provider legitimately sees the breakdown that decides their own earnings. That half is recorded as
a deliberate divergence in the waiver file rather than pretended away.

## W.4 — `Money((major * 100).round(), code)` on two money-out screens (High, fixed, Kimi's layer)

**What.** Three sites converted a typed major-unit amount to minor units with a hardcoded ×100:
`wallet_page.dart` (**withdrawal**), and `provider_tools_page.dart` twice (earnings goal and
**instant payout**).

**Consequence.** In a zero-exponent currency — UGX, XOF, XAF, RWF, and six more in the table — a
provider typing `50000` asks to withdraw 5,000,000 minor units. The server refuses it against
their real balance, so **no one can withdraw at all in those currencies**, and the message they
get is that they have insufficient funds while looking at a balance that plainly covers it.

**This is M3.20 again.** That finding was the same arithmetic in the request form, fixed on
2026-09-18 with a comment explaining why. The comment stayed in the request form; the pattern did
not spread to the screens written afterwards.

**Fix.** `Money.fromMajorAmount(double, currencyCode)` in `suskii_domain`, which multiplies by
`10^exponent`, and the three call sites now use it. It takes a double because the entry field is
text and `12.50` in a two-exponent currency must survive — `fromMajorUnits(int)` already existed
and would have rounded that to `13`, which is why it was not the answer. A regression test covers
UGX, NGN fractional, USD, and rounding in XOF.

**Fixed in Kimi's layer rather than filed**, because it is contained (one new factory, three call
sites), unambiguous, and money-losing. The fix is uncommitted here, alongside Kimi's own work — see
"What is not committed" below.

## W.5 — the three web apps have no CI at all (Medium, filed, Kimi's file)

**What.** `.github/workflows/frontend-ci.yaml` runs pub get, l10n codegen, build_runner, format,
`flutter analyze packages apps/mobile`, and the Dart tests including the dart2js run. It does not
contain the strings `npm`, `next` or `web-customer`.

**Consequence.** `apps/web-customer` (18 pages), `apps/web-marketing` (13) and `apps/web-admin`
(19) — about fifty pages of TypeScript and React, a third of the frontend — are verified by nobody.
A broken build lands on `main` and is found when a person next runs it by hand. Kimi's M8.5 note
says all three build, and that was true when they ran it; nothing keeps it true.

**Filed, not fixed.** `frontend-ci.yaml` is Kimi's file under the ownership table, and the shape of
the fix is theirs to choose (one job or three, whether to add `tsc --noEmit` and a linter, whether
to cache). The finding is precise enough to act on without a conversation.

## The control this pass was really about

`supabase/tools/check_rls_matrix.py`, wired into `backend-db.yaml`. It checks five things: every
function the matrix names exists; every table it gives a row to exists; a cell naming specific
columns has a column-restricted grant; an `R:scope` cell has a policy that reads an admin scope;
an `R:part` cell has a policy that tests participation.

Rules C, D and E are heuristics over policy text and are wrong sometimes. That is what
`rls-matrix-waivers.json` is for, and it is the half that was missing: **a divergence is now either
fixed or written down with a reason a stranger can evaluate.** Eighteen waivers, each naming what
diverges, why it is acceptable, and what to do about it. The checker also warns when a waiver stops
matching anything, because a waiver for something that no longer diverges is its own small lie.

Two bugs in the checker itself were found and fixed while building it, and both are worth recording
because they are the same shape as what it hunts:

- The policy-body regex anchored on a `;` that `split_statements` had already removed, so it
  matched **one** policy in the whole repository — and that one was `realtime.messages` from inside
  a dollar-quoted `EXECUTE`, mis-attributed to `public.messages`. Rules D and E were silently dead
  and produced one false positive. A checker that reports nothing looks exactly like a codebase
  with nothing wrong.
- The stale-waiver comparison ran against findings *after* waived ones were filtered out, so every
  waiver looked stale.
