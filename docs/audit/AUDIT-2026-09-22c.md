# Audit — 2026-09-22 (third pass) — the Participant column

Not a scheduled pass. Kimi delivered M8.5 on 2026-09-21 — a map of all 82 shipped routes to PRD
story IDs — and started M8.6, the provider job-execution layer that the map named as the largest
product gap. Reviewing their domain and mock work against the backend it will be wired to at M9
found that **the backend cannot serve it**.

One finding, in four limbs. It is the third instance this week of one failure mode, and the
pattern is now worth naming more loudly than the individual bugs.

Fixed in `supabase/migrations/20260922121300_participant_reads.sql`; 29 assertions in
`supabase/tests/database/44_participant_reads_test.sql`.

## The pattern, stated once

| Pass | Finding | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| 2026-09-22 (2nd) | U.1 | The matrix defines `R:scope` on ~50 cells. One function read `country_scope`. |

Three times now: the matrix specifies an access, part of it was never implemented, and no test
caught it **because no screen had asked yet**. The RLS tests are thorough about the accesses that
exist and silent about the ones that were specified and skipped — a deny test passes whether the
denial is correct or whether the allow clause was simply never written. The two are
indistinguishable from inside the suite.

The one control that would have caught all three is a check that every cell of the matrix is
either implemented or explicitly waived. That does not exist and is not built here; it is
recorded as the obvious follow-up.

## V.1 — the Participant column was never implemented (High, fixed)

**What.** Section 5 of `docs/plan/rls-policy-matrix.md` has seven columns, one of them
**Participant**. `requests` carries `R:part` in it; so does `request_media`. Neither policy has
ever had a participant clause:

- `requests_read_own` — `customer_id = auth.uid()` or scoped support. No participant.
- `request_media_read_own` — customer only. No participant, and no staff either.

The Provider column says a provider meets the market "via F (`provider_feed`, PostGIS) — never a
table select, so a provider cannot enumerate all requests", and "via feed card" for the media.
Both are correct and both were built. They describe the provider **before** they are assigned.
After assignment, nothing widened.

**Consequence.** An assigned provider could read `jobs` (their agreed amount, their timestamps,
the PIN flags) and `get_job_summary` (a status, a category key, a next-step key). They could not
read `description`, `pickup_label`, `destination_label`, either landmark note, `urgency`,
`declared_value_minor`, or a single one of the customer's photos.

**A provider who accepted a delivery could not see the address.** The core loop of the product
could not complete against this schema, and the reason it was never noticed is that
`/provider/jobs` in the mobile app is still an M1 empty-state stub — the first screen that would
have asked for it is the one Kimi started building this week.

**Fix, and the four limbs.**

*(a) The assigned provider reads the request.* Through `private.is_assigned_job_provider`, which
gates on `jobs.assigned_at` rather than on `requests.status`. Two reasons, and they are the whole
design of the finding:

- `assigned_at` is only ever set in the `paid_held → assigned` transition, which refuses to run
  from any other status. It therefore means *funded*, not merely *agreed*. Without that gate, a
  provider could bid, have the offer accepted, read the exact address and walk away before the
  customer paid — an address harvest with no cost and no trace. Threat model R-05 (off-platform
  cash deals) is the highest-rated business risk on that table.
- It is monotonic. A status test would have to enumerate the states that count, and `cancelled`
  is reachable from **both** sides of payment: a job cancelled before funding would satisfy a
  naive `status <> 'agreed'` test and hand the address over on the way out. Once set,
  `assigned_at` stays set, so the provider keeps the record of a job they actually did — which
  they need for their history, a rating and a dispute. The test asserts the cancel case directly.

*(b) The matched provider reads the request's media rows.* Narrower than it first looked, and
worth recording as a correction. The first draft of this fix claimed a bidding provider could not
fetch the objects the feed card lists, and replaced `private.may_read_request_media` accordingly.
That was wrong. **Phase 3 already got the storage side right** -- `...120500_ratings_and_reputation`
widened that function to the same test the feed applies (active, same country, registered for the
category, request still open), and `16_ratings_test` has asserted it since. The replacement
clobbered a wider definition with a narrower one, and CI caught it on the first run. The function
is left alone.

What is real is smaller: the **rows** in `public.request_media` were customer-only while the
**objects** they describe were reachable, so the table and the bucket disagreed.
`private.is_matched_provider` (the provider has an offer thread on this request) closes that for a
bidder and `is_job_participant` for the assigned provider. The storage function stays deliberately
wider, because the feed hands a provider `media_paths` directly and never needs a table read first.

*(c) `request_media` gains the staff scope.* S.1 scoped the `request-media` **storage objects**
by ticket or case on 2026-09-22 and left the **rows that describe them** customer-only. Support
and dispute now reach the row on the same terms they reach the object, and super admin reads it.

*(d) `job_events` was already broken for providers.* Found while fixing (a), and it would have
survived (a) untouched. Its provider clause is nested inside a subquery on `public.requests`:

```sql
USING (EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM public.requests r
               WHERE r.id = job_events.request_id
                 AND (r.customer_id = auth.uid()
                      OR EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM public.jobs j ...provider...))))
```

A policy's subqueries are evaluated with the caller's own permissions, so that inner
`SELECT … FROM public.requests` is itself filtered by `requests_read_own`. The provider clause
could only ever be true for somebody who could already read the request — which, before this
migration, no provider could. **The clause has never once evaluated true.** It now calls
`private.is_job_participant`, the SECURITY DEFINER helper that `chat-media` and `job-proofs`
already use.

A sweep for the same shape across every policy that references `public.requests` found twelve
policies and **this was the only one**: `offers_read`, `jobs_read_participant`,
`proofs_read_participant`, `offer_threads_read` and `location_samples_read` all put the provider
clause on the table's own columns and use the `requests` subquery only for the customer clause,
which is safe because a customer can always read their own request.

**Tests.** 29 assertions, and the deny half carries the weight. Three providers exist in the
fixture for it: one assigned, one who bid and lost, one whose offer was accepted but whose
customer never paid. The last two read nothing, before and after the request is cancelled.

## Not fixed here

| Item | Why |
|---|---|
| `saved_places` and `reveal_access_note(request_id)` | The matrix (§4) specifies both — "access note via `reveal_access_note` for the assigned provider **during an active job only**". Neither exists. That is a feature, not a policy gap, and it is the matrix's own precedent for time-boxing the most sensitive location detail. It should be built before the first live country. |
| Exact addresses in a provider's job history | (a) leaves the address readable after the job ends, because a table SELECT policy cannot hide one column from one role and cutting the read at completion would break history, ratings and the dispute window. Whether a provider's app should retain a customer's exact address indefinitely is a DPIA question, not an RLS one. **Raised, not decided.** |
| `dispute_officer` on `requests` | The matrix gives it `R:scope`; the policy has `support_agent` only. Left alone deliberately — S.1 and U.1 settled that dispute reads go through `private.dispute_scope`, and widening the plain role here would undo that. |
| A matrix-coverage check in CI | The control that would have caught U.1, U.3 and V.1. Needs the matrix in a machine-readable form; `check_allowlist.py` is the precedent for the shape. |
